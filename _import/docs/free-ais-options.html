Free AIS / GPS tracking for the Kuwait fleet

What actually exists, what doesn't, and the one route that is genuinely free forever — researched 29 July 2026

The one-line answer There is no free AIS data service that covers Kuwait. Not one. But you can get free-forever coverage by putting a $200 antenna on a roof in Shuaiba — and the numbers say a single receiver covers your entire operation.

Why free APIs don't work here

Free ship-tracking services are not charities running satellites. They are swap clubs. Hobbyists and ports around the world buy a small radio receiver, point it at the sea, and upload what they hear. In exchange they get to see everyone else's feed. The data is free because volunteers collected it.

That model has one fatal weakness: it only covers places where volunteers live. Northern Europe is blanketed. The US coasts are blanketed. The Persian Gulf has almost nobody — no hobbyist community, and the ports don't share publicly.

So the Gulf is a hole in every free network. This isn't a matter of finding the right provider; the data simply isn't being collected by anyone who gives it away.

Global Fishing Watch Free API Partly Fishing vessels only, and delayed. Your boats are supply and crew vessels. Wrong dataset.

Free satellite AIS does not exist Satellite AIS is the only technology that sees ships anywhere on earth without a local antenna. Every operator of it (Spire, Kpler, ORBCOMM) sells to shipping companies and governments at enterprise rates. There is no free tier, and there won't be.

The route that is actually free: your own receiver

AIS is just an unencrypted VHF radio signal. Every ship broadcasts its position in the clear, continuously, for anyone to hear. Nobody owns it and nobody bills for it. A $200 antenna on a roof in Shuaiba receives your vessels' own broadcasts directly, and after that the data costs nothing per month, forever.

Why this works unusually well for your fleet

The catch with a receiver is range — it only hears ships within line of sight, typically 15–20 nautical miles with the antenna 15 m up. That kills the idea for most fleets. It does not kill it for yours, because your fleet barely leaves the neighbourhood. Measured from Shuaiba Berth 4 against the coordinates in data/locations.json:

Compare against the paid options: Datalastic is €199/month (€2,388/year) and MyShipTracking €90/month (€1,080/year). The receiver pays for itself in under six weeks against Datalastic, or about ten weeks against MyShipTracking.

What it gives you beyond free

  • Better data than you were paying for. Datalastic polled roughly every 30 minutes. Your own receiver hears each vessel every few seconds — the raw stream, not snapshots. That is exactly the continuous feed docs/streaming-tracking-plan.md was designed around, and it makes the smooth-motion and trail work in that plan worth doing.
  • No credit limits. No metering, no overage, no watching LIVE_DEFAULT_INTERVAL_MS in case it burns 86,000 credits a month.
  • AISHub for free as a bonus. Once the receiver is running and uploading, you meet AISHub's contributor bar and get their worldwide API at no cost — useful if a vessel ever sails outside Kuwait.
  • It's yours. No trial expiring silently, which is exactly what took the map down on 21 May.

The honest downsides

  • Somebody has to mount an antenna. It needs a spot with a clear view of the water, mains power, and internet. A Shuaiba office roof or a colleague's flat facing the port. This is the real obstacle — not the money or the code.
  • Roughly a day of code. A small Node service reads the receiver's NMEA stream and writes the same data/ais-history/ JSON the app already consumes. Phase 1 of the streaming plan, already scoped.
  • Single point of failure. If the Pi or the internet drops, tracking stops until someone reboots it. A paid API has no such dependency.
  • Hard ceiling at the horizon. If a vessel is ever chartered far up the Gulf, it vanishes until it returns. Today's routes never do that.

Recommendation

  1. Stop looking for a free API. It has now been searched twice and live-tested. For Kuwait it does not exist. The finding is settled — this document is here so it doesn't get researched a third time.
  2. Answer one question: can you get an antenna onto a roof near Shuaiba port with power and internet? Everything hinges on this, and it's an operational question, not a technical one.
  3. If yes → buy the receiver. ~$200 once, $0/month, better data than Datalastic ever gave, and it pays for itself in six weeks.
  4. If no → the honest answer is you must pay. MyShipTracking at €90/month is the cheaper of the two finalists and adequate for Kuwait nearshore work; it needs about a day of adapter code. Datalastic at €199/month works with the code as-is. Both have free trials — probe either with tools/validate_ais_provider.py before committing.
  5. Meanwhile the map still works. Positions are estimated from the captains' daily reports. Less precise, never blank.

Already done Charlie 3's identity is recorded and merged: AIS_MMSI_CH3=341930000 in .env, plus MMSI and IMO 9889928 in data/vessels.json, .env.example, and the import tool's vessel list. Whichever route you pick, Charlie 3 starts being tracked with no further setup.

One caveat carried in the code comments: public trackers describe MMSI 341930000 as an offshore tug/supply vessel built 2020, while your Charlie 3 works as a fast crew boat. Probably the same hull under a loose classification — but check the first real fixes against its daily reports with tools/crosscheck_reports.py before trusting the feed.
